## Genre and Mood

**Gabin** made **Doo uap, doo uap, doo uap** where nu-jazz, house and lounge meet: a jazz standard from the 1930s, a house pulse and the polish of a hotel bar. It is groovy and playful, and XLR8R praised its delightful kitsch; the joke is that it takes one of jazz's best-known songs about swing and sets it to a house beat.

## Song Summary

The song underneath is Duke Ellington's It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing), and the credits still name Ellington and his lyricist Irving Mills as its writers. XLR8R heard the big-band classic sampled; the Minnesota Daily heard Ellington's big band jazz filtered through modern dance rhythms, and explained the title as Italian for doo-wop. This is the album version, over seven minutes long, credited with the saxophonist Stefano Di Battista as featured artist.

## Concepts in Focus

{{ <concept_link name="sampling" /> }} is how much dance music talks to older records: a fragment of a recording becomes material for a new one. Here the source is one of the best-known titles in jazz. The credits keep Ellington and Mills, because the song is still theirs.

{{ <concept_link name="swing" /> }} is the subject of the original title and the tension of this track. Ellington's 1932 recording, sung by Ivie Anderson, contains one of the earliest uses of the word swing in popular music, meaning the long-short lilt of jazz rhythm. House music runs on an even, steady pulse. Listen for whether the jazz loosens the beat or the beat straightens the jazz.

As a {{ <concept_link name="featured artist" /> }}, Di Battista brings a jazz musician's name into a DJ's record: a saxophonist born in Rome in 1969, who toured with the Elvin Jones Jazz Machine and recorded for Blue Note.

## Artistic Context

Gabin are the bassist Max Bottini, who played jazz with John Scofield and Billy Cobham, and the DJ and programmer Filippo Clary, who spent much of the 1990s playing chill rooms in Rome. They named the duo after the French actor Jean Gabin. Their debut album Gabin came out in Europe on Virgin in 2002 and in the US on Astralwerks, with this track among its singles; XLR8R heard the album as an Italian answer to St Germain.

## Impact and Reception

The single reached number three in Italy, the duo's closest brush with the mainstream. The Minnesota Daily called it the album's standout, though it named the disc's one flaw a horrific misuse of Blue Note alumni saxophones. It also travelled through compilations, from the Hôtel Costes series onwards, and its video featured a Citroën, like the album cover. Put on the long version and decide whether it swings.
