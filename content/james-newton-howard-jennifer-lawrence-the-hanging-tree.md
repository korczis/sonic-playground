## Genre and Mood

**James Newton Howard** and **Jennifer Lawrence** turned **The Hanging Tree** from a scene in a film into a hit, a folk ballad that grows into a soundtrack anthem. The mood is haunting, melancholic and quietly defiant, an eerie old-time song that becomes a call to arms.

## Song Summary

The lyrics come from Suzanne Collins' novel Mockingjay, a dark, riddling folk song about a meeting at a hanging tree at midnight. In The Hunger Games: Mockingjay, Part 1, Katniss Everdeen sings it by a river, and the film then turns it into a rebel hymn sung by crowds of people marching on a dam. The recording follows that arc: it starts with Lawrence's bare, unpolished voice and grows into a swell of choir, strings and pounding drums. That rawness is the point; she sounds like a person, not a star.

## Musical Breakdown

The track is a slow crescendo, moving from a lone voice to a massed, cinematic climax across three and a half minutes.

### Intro

Lawrence sings alone, unaccompanied and close to the microphone, her voice plain and a little rough, setting the song's chill immediately.

### Verse 1

The first verses keep the intimacy of a lullaby, the melody simple and repetitive, the words strange and unsettling.

### Build-up

Other voices begin to join her, and low strings and percussion enter underneath, turning a private song into a shared one.

### Climax

James Newton Howard's orchestration surges: martial drums, swelling strings and a choir carry the refrain, transforming a whispered folk tune into a defiant anthem.

### Outro

The music pulls back and the song ends close to where it began, the unsettling melody hanging in the silence.

## Artistic Context

James Newton Howard scored all four Hunger Games films, and The Hanging Tree appeared in 2014 with the release of Mockingjay, Part 1. Collins' lyrics were set to music by Jeremiah Fraites and Wesley Schultz of the folk-rock band The Lumineers, whose plain, stomping style suits the song, and Howard built the arrangement around Lawrence's voice.

## Impact and Reception

The song debuted and peaked at number 12 on the Billboard Hot 100, the highest-charting song from the franchise, and reached the top 20 in the UK, remarkable for an actress who has said she dislikes singing. It inspired countless covers and remixes. It is here because it shows how a simple, haunting melody can outgrow its film. Listen as it builds and feel the room change.
