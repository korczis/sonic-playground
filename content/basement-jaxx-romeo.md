## Genre and Mood

**Basement Jaxx** opened their second album with **Romeo**, and it plays like a summary of what the English duo could do in 2001: house, UK garage and pop folded into three and a half minutes. It is catchy and energetic on the surface, bittersweet underneath.

## Song Summary

The British R&B singer Kele Le Roc sings the lead, with Corryne Dwyer on backing vocals, in what Vice called her jilted lover guise. Underneath her, Felix Buxton and Simon Ratcliffe set a sample of Cloud One's 1981 disco record Don't Let My Rainbow Pass Me By. Tim Finney of Freaky Trigger heard a punchy vocal house track drawing equally on contemporary R&B's robosoul and 2-step's cheery rhythmic friction, and called it gorgeous pop perfection, not to mention confection.

## Musical Breakdown

No source walks through the track bar by bar; these parts follow what the reviews single out.

### The Groove

The beat never lets up. Vice heard all forward motion, a relentless percussive machine that pushes and pushes, and Finney the stutter of 2-step garage.

### Verses and Synths

Le Roc's vocal sits right at the front, disconcertingly direct to Finney, while synths flare up around it: trance-like oscillations in his hearing, cheesy new wave synths in PopMatters'.

### Middle Eight

For Vice the best moment comes here, when Le Roc finally lets loose before the original melody returns.

### Outro

In its final ten seconds, in Vice's words, the track simply crumbles away.

## Concepts in Focus

Romeo shows {{ <concept_link name="sampling" /> }} at work in dance pop. A disco record from 1981 sits inside a groove that critics heard as garage and house, linking two dance eras.

It is also a lesson in the {{ <concept_link name="featured artist" /> }}. Basement Jaxx were producers first: an early version had Buxton singing, but the single went to Kele Le Roc, one of several guest voices on Rooty, and her performance is what reviewers argue about.

The {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }} recasts the song as a tribute to 1960s and 1970s Bollywood films. It opens on a billboard for a Romeo blockbuster; the Indian actress Divya Dutta, in a sari, is torn between two men, there are tears, a fight and a car chase, and it ends with a dance in front of a temple at sunset.

## Artistic Context

Romeo was one of the first songs Basement Jaxx wrote and produced for Rooty, held back because they had just released the compilation Jaxx Unreleased. It came out on XL Recordings on 4 June 2001, three weeks before the album, which took its name from the duo's club night at a small Brixton bar. The next album opened the same way, with a guest singer on {{ <track_link source="basement-jaxx-good-luck.md" /> }}.

## Impact and Reception

The single entered the UK chart at number six, topped the UK dance and independent charts and reached number five on the US dance club chart. Critics split: the BBC called it still a bittersweet pop classic, Pitchfork's review found it creepily Janet-esque, and Pitchfork later ranked it 50th among the tracks of the 2000s. Start it, and see whether it carries you or wears you down.
