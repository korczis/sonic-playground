## Genre and Mood

**Daft Punk** made **Around the World** out of almost nothing, and it became one of the founding records of French house. House and electronic minimalism meet a funky, bouncing bassline in a mood that is hypnotic, groovy and iconic: just over seven minutes on the album that feel like a single, endless, perfectly timed loop.

## Song Summary

The lyric is the title, sung by a machine-processed voice again and again: 144 times on the album version and 80 times on the radio edit. Around it the duo builds a groove from a rubbery bassline, a crisp drum machine, filtered keyboard riffs and a few disco-flavoured accents. Daft Punk summed up the method themselves: it was "like making a Chic record with a talk box and just playing the bass on the synthesizer". The trick is that the repetition never feels static; small changes in filter, layering and emphasis keep pulling your ear back in.

## Musical Breakdown

### Intro

Muffled, filtered drums and bass emerge as if heard from outside the club, the sound slowly opening up as you walk through the door.

### Build-up

The bassline steps forward in full, round and elastic, and the beat locks in with it. It is already irresistible, and the vocal has not even started.

### Main Section

The robot voice begins its chant over and over, riding the groove like another instrument rather than a singer.

### Bridge

Mid-track the arrangement shifts its weight, the bass and keyboard riffs trading places, changing the colour without breaking the spell.

### Climax

All the elements lock together at full strength, the filter wide open and the chant relentless, a peak reached through accumulation rather than any sudden drop.

### Outro

The track slowly pulls its layers back, closing the filter until the groove fades away like the club door swinging shut behind you.

## Concepts in Focus

Around the World is the textbook {{ <concept_link name="loop" /> }}: a few bars of bass, drums and voice repeated for seven minutes. What keeps it alive is arrangement, not new material. Parts enter and leave, swap emphasis and return, so the loop is the same but the music around it is not. Listen to one element at a time, the bass, then the high keyboard, then the drums, and you will hear how differently each one behaves over the length of the track.

The voice is the second lesson. The title is sung through a {{ <concept_link name="vocoder" /> }} and talk box, devices that shape one sound with the character of another so a human voice comes out sounding like a machine. That robotic tone removes personality from the singer and makes the phrase another riff, which is exactly why it can be repeated 144 times without tiring. Compare {{ <track_link source="daft-punk-harder-better-faster-stronger.md" /> }}, where the duo pushes the same idea into a list of commands.

The third tool is the {{ <concept_link name="filter sweep" /> }}. In the intro and outro the sound is muffled, as if behind a wall, and gradually opens or closes; a filter is cutting the high frequencies and then letting them back in. French house made this its signature: it raises and lowers energy without changing a single note. Finally, the {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }} by Michel Gondry, choreographed by Blanca Li, makes the arrangement visible: robots are the vocals, tall athletes the bass, "disco girls" the high keyboard, skeletons the guitar and mummies the drum machine, each group moving only to its own part.

## Artistic Context

The song comes from *Homework*, the 1997 debut album by Thomas Bangalter and Guy-Manuel de Homem-Christo on Virgin, which helped put French house on the global map. It was the album's second single, released in April 1997 after Indo Silver Club and before Burnin'. Gondry himself pointed out how close its bassline is to Chic's Good Times, the disco record behind so much house music.

## Impact and Reception

"Around the World" was a major international hit: number 5 in the United Kingdom and in France, number 1 on the UK dance chart and on the US Dance Club Songs chart, and platinum in the UK. NME placed it 21st among the 150 best tracks of the preceding fifteen years in 2011, and it remains one of the defining electronic tracks of the nineties, still played everywhere from festival main stages to wedding parties. It is on this playground as proof that minimalism can be maximal fun. Put it on, let the bassline take over and count how many times you hear the title before you stop counting.
