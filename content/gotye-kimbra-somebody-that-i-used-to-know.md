## Genre and Mood

**Gotye**'s **Somebody That I Used To Know**, sung with **Kimbra**, is a mid-tempo art-pop and alternative-rock ballad about the end of a relationship. Bittersweet rather than bitter, its power is restraint: Sound On Sound described a song that stays understated until the dramatic entrance of its first chorus, a minute and a half in.

## Song Summary

The lyric tells a break-up from both sides. Gotye remembers the relationship and the way it ended; Kimbra answers with her side of the story. The music is a collage of a sample of Luiz Bonfá's 1967 instrumental Seville, drums, a xylophone melody based on Baa, Baa, Black Sheep, flutes and synth, with Lucas Taranto on bass. Gotye wrote, played and produced it from January to May 2011 in The Barn, his studio on his parents' property on the Mornington Peninsula.

## Musical Breakdown

### Intro

The song opens on the Bonfá guitar, which Gotye cut into a repeating two-note loop he found hypnotic. Vinyl crackle is left on the first phrases only, like a record starting.

### Verse 1

Gotye's verses are deliberately quiet, carried by the loop, the xylophone and a wobbly guitar line between them whose vibrato came from Gotye moving the turntable by hand.

### Chorus

The first chorus enters dramatically. Bass, kick and a driven copy of the guitar arrive together, the vocals are doubled, and Gotye's voice climbs high.

### Verse 2: Kimbra

Gotye got stuck after the first chorus; it felt weak to him. A third writing session gave the woman her own verse. A bell-like autoharp tone introduces her, and her lines turn the story around.

### Outro

The final section stacks backing vocals; the closing "ohs" are Gotye's demo takes, mixed loudly so they battle with his lead.

## Concepts in Focus

The song is built on {{ <concept_link name="sampling" /> }}: two downbeats and two offbeats of Seville. Even the xylophone and the wobbly guitar are notes chopped from records and re-pitched. The Bonfá sample is about 30 cents flat, and correcting it made the track sound too straight, so it stayed.

At the end of Kimbra's verse the bass stops alternating and holds one note for several bars, a {{ <concept_link name="pedal point" /> }}. Gary Ewer hears the song's chords keep pulling back to D minor without reaching F major, music that wants to move on and cannot.

In the chorus, a compressor on the guitar is triggered by the kick drum, a form of {{ <concept_link name="sidechain compression" /> }}: Tétaz describes the kick's phrasing pumping the guitar. The {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }} by Natasha Pincus paints both singers into the backdrop in stop-motion, from an artwork by Gotye's father. Compare the restraint of {{ <track_link source="alt-j-breezeblocks.md" /> }}.

## Artistic Context

Gotye is Wally De Backer, a Belgian-Australian singer-songwriter; Kimbra is a New Zealand singer whom engineer François Tétaz recommended after another singer cancelled. The song was released on 5 July 2011 through Eleven, a single from his third album, Making Mirrors.

## Impact and Reception

It reached number one in more than 23 national charts, Australia and the UK among them, and spent eight weeks at number one on the Billboard Hot 100. It won two Grammy Awards, including Record of the Year. Listen for the minute and a half before the chorus.
