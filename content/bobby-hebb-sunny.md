## Genre and Mood

**Bobby Hebb** wrote **Sunny** in 1963, and Wikipedia calls it a soul jazz standard; his own recording crossed into the R&B and pop charts in 1966. It is warm and uplifting on the surface and bittersweet underneath: a thank-you to the sun written in the shadow of a death.

## Song Summary

Hebb's older brother Harold was killed in a knife fight outside a Nashville nightclub in November 1963, the day after President Kennedy was assassinated. Many critics hear the song as a response; Hebb himself disputed that story and said he simply preferred a sunny disposition to a lousy one, "looking for a brighter day". Producer Jerry Ross recorded it at Bell Sound Studios in New York with an arrangement by Joe Renzetti, during a session already in overtime, after many of the booked musicians had left for other dates.

## Musical Breakdown

### Verse 1

The song is built on a sixteen-bar cycle that starts on E minor and repeats a four-bar phrase twice: Em7, G7, Cmaj7, then F♯m7 and B7 to turn back. Trumpets, trombone and saxophones, piano, two guitars, two basses, drums and percussion are credited on the session.

### Main Section

The third phrase replaces B7 with F7, a jazz substitution, before a closing ii–V–i settles on the tonic. After the second cycle the whole form moves up a half step every time round, so the song keeps rising.

### Outro

There is no final chord to resolve it: the record ends on a looped tag.

Elements of it turn up in Sly and the Family Stone's If You Want Me to Stay and Stevie Wonder's Don't You Worry 'bout a Thing; Freddie Hubbard's Red Clay loops its first four bars, and Grover Washington Jr.'s Just the Two of Us reorders them at double time. ## Artistic Context

Bobby Hebb was born in Nashville in 1938 to two blind musicians and performed with Harold as a song-and-dance duo from the age of three. He played spoons in Roy Acuff's band and sang backup on Bo Diddley's Diddley Daddy. Sunny opens his 1966 Philips album of the same name, produced by Ross and arranged by Renzetti.

## Impact and Reception

The single reached number 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 in late August 1966, behind Summer in the City, as well as number 3 on the R&B chart and 12 in the UK, and its success put Hebb on tour with the Beatles that year.
