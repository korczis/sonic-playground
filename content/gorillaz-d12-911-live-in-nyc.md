## Genre and Mood

**Gorillaz** and **D12** made **911** as rap rock, and this recording catches it on a stage in New York: a hip-hop crew and an alternative band in one room, intense, dark and raw. It is a live document rather than a polished single, eight minutes long, from one of the very few nights the song was played.

## Song Summary

911 began by accident. D12, the Detroit hip-hop crew, were stranded in England by the September 11 attacks, and on 12 September 2001 they recorded the song with Damon Albarn at his West London studio, without Eminem. Albarn had long wanted to try something drawn from Middle-Eastern music, and Terry Hall of the Specials, who had been taking lessons from a Middle-Eastern singer, sang the chorus in harmony with him. The studio version came out in December 2001 on the Bad Company soundtrack. This recording is the song's live debut, at the Hammerstein Ballroom in New York on 28 February 2002.

## Musical Breakdown

No review or timed guide describes this performance; what follows is what the sources say about the song and the night, and the rest is for your ears.

### The Band

On the 2001-2002 tour Gorillaz played behind a large cinema screen showing their videos: Albarn on vocals and keyboards, Simon Katz on guitar, Cass Browne on drums, Mike Smith on keyboards, DJ D-Zire on turntables and, in North America, Roberto Occhipinti on bass.

### Verses and Chorus

D12 joined them for this song in New York only, trading verses between the choruses; the fan wiki also lists Terry Hall among the performers.

### Jump the Gut

The 8:04 release keeps what followed: a short G-Bite, Jump the Gut, played straight after 911.

## Concepts in Focus

A {{ <concept_link name="live recording" /> }} preserves a night rather than a version. This one was released on 25 November 2011, nearly ten years after the show, during the band's tenth-anniversary radio shows on Spotify, hosted by their cartoon bassist Murdoc. A shorter edit fades out into his commentary instead of the G-Bite.

911 is also a {{ <concept_link name="posse cut" /> }}: the verses pass through D12's members, whose names fill its writing credits next to Albarn, Jamie Hewlett and Hall. Their {{ <concept_link name="rapping" /> }} carries the song between choruses. Hear the crew in its own setting in {{ <track_link source="d12-devils-night.md" /> }}.

## Artistic Context

In 2002 Gorillaz were a cartoon band whose real musicians stood behind a screen; Albarn later said he was frustrated back there. 911 was offered as a free download from the Gorillaz website, and its video reused the visuals of Clint Eastwood and {{ <track_link source="gorillaz-19-2000.md" /> }} with a new background. Years later an unused freestyle by Proof from the London sessions resurfaced on The Manifesto, from the 2026 album The Mountain.

## Impact and Reception

911 stayed a rarity. After New York it was played once more in 2002, in Lisbon with Phi Life Cypher rapping, and then not for 24 years, until Kuniva and Swifty McVay joined Gorillaz in Detroit on 7 October 2026, Swifty McVay saying "Rest in peace, Proof!" for their late bandmate as he arrived. Listen to this one as the first time the song met an audience.
