## Genre and Mood

**Klyne** keep things small and close on **Entropy**, a slice of electronic soul that drifts at downtempo pace. It is dreamy in the way a half-remembered summer evening is dreamy, with a melancholic undertow that never tips into gloom, and it is intimate enough that the vocal seems to be sung a few inches from your ear rather than projected at a room.

## Song Summary

The title promises disorder, but the track is all restraint. A soft, falsetto-leaning vocal circles a feeling of something slowly coming apart, while the production stays deliberately sparse: muted keys, a supple, slightly rubbery bassline and a beat that sits back in the pocket instead of pushing forward. The memorable detail is the space itself. Klyne leave gaps between every element, and the song breathes through them.

## Musical Breakdown

Entropy unfolds gently, adding and removing layers rather than building to a big drop.

### Intro

Hazy chords and a soft pulse set a twilight tone, as if the track has already been playing in another room and you have just opened the door.

### Verse 1

The voice enters light and unhurried, floating above the keys with a hint of reverb. The beat stays minimal, giving every phrase room to land.

### Chorus

The hook lifts by a few degrees rather than exploding: harmonies stack up, the bass grows rounder and the melody leans into its most yearning notes.

### Bridge

For a moment the arrangement thins almost to nothing, leaving the vocal and a few glassy textures suspended, a small pause that makes the return of the groove feel warmer.

### Outro

The elements peel away one by one until only the soft chords remain, letting the song dissolve rather than end, which suits its title perfectly.

## Concepts in Focus

Nick Klein's voice sits high and light, leaning into {{ <concept_link name="falsetto" /> }}, the register above the normal chest voice. Falsetto has less power and more air, which is why it sounds intimate rather than declamatory: it reads as something confided, not announced. Much of the electronic soul of the mid-2010s, from Klyne to the artists they were compared with, used it this way, and it is the main reason Entropy feels so close to the ear.

The production around the voice is built from a few {{ <concept_link name="synthesizer" /> }} parts and a lot of space. Hypebeast described tropical-leaning beats and pulsing synths, and Ferdous Dehzad keeps each element sparse so that silence becomes part of the arrangement. The Fader reported that the song began a year earlier as a raw, psychedelic version full of piano and guitar and was reshaped into this simpler summer pop; what was removed matters as much as what remains. Hear the stripped-back other side of the single, {{ <track_link source="klyne-paralyzed.md" /> }}.

## Artistic Context

Klyne are the Dutch duo of singer Nick Klein and producer Ferdous Dehzad, who grew up in the south of the Netherlands, in a village near Eindhoven, with Indonesian and Afghan roots respectively, and started making music together as teenagers. Paralyzed first appeared on SoundCloud in early 2015 under Klein's name before the pair renamed themselves Klyne to represent them both. On 7 August 2015 the British label Aesop released Paralyzed as their debut single, with Entropy as its double A-side partner, a pairing that introduced their blend of R&B-tinged vocals, pop melody and understated electronic production.

## Impact and Reception

Entropy arrived to warm notice from music blogs: The Fader framed Klyne as makers of mellow summer pop and called them a long-lost cousin of Jai Paul, and Hypebeast and Complex covered the track within days. Its partner Paralyzed went viral, and the single established the duo before their self-titled album on Because Music in 2017, which brought their second viral hit, Water Flow. It belongs on this playground as a perfect late-night track: unshowy, carefully produced and quietly addictive. Put it on when the day is winding down, turn the lights low and let it slowly take everything apart.
