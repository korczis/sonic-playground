## Genre and Mood

**DJ Patife**'s remix of **Sambassim** is drum and bass with a Brazilian heart, a track where bossa nova softness and São Paulo club energy meet halfway. It is vibrant, groovy and openly joyful, the sound of a beach afternoon sped up to drum and bass tempo without losing any of its ease.

## Song Summary

Sambassim began as a song by the Brazilian singer, multi-instrumentalist and composer Fernanda Porto, a pioneer of what became known as drum 'n' bossa, and Patife's version turns her airy, sing-song melody into the centrepiece of a rolling breakbeat. The trick is contrast. The drums skip and rattle at full speed while the vocal floats on top as if nothing were rushing at all, and the bass rolls underneath like a slow tide. It is music for dancing that never forgets it is also a song.

## Musical Breakdown

The remix is built in the classic liquid drum and bass shape: a melodic introduction, a drop into the break, and long, flowing stretches where the groove and the voice trade places.

### Intro

Warm chords and a hint of Porto's melody set a relaxed, sunlit scene, the kind of opening that could just as easily lead into a bossa nova ballad.

### Build-up

Percussion starts to gather, light and crisp, with samba-flavoured hits hinting at the speed to come while the harmony stays soft.

### Main Section

The break drops and the track finds its stride: quick, shuffling drums, a deep, round bassline and the vocal gliding above them in Portuguese, never straining to keep up.

### Bridge

The drums thin out for a moment and the melody gets space to breathe, a short exhale before the rhythm section rolls back in.

### Climax

Everything returns together, the bass at its fullest and the percussion at its busiest, the samba swing and the breakbeat finally sounding like one idea.

### Outro

The track eases back towards its gentle opening, leaving the melody hanging in the air as the drums fall away.

## Concepts in Focus

This is a {{ <concept_link name="remix" /> }} that became more famous than the song's first form. Patife kept Porto's vocal and melody and rebuilt everything underneath, moving the song from samba-tinged Brazilian pop into the drum and bass club. A German promoter's biography of Porto notes that the Patife version was a club hit before her own album appeared, and Patife himself said that after this remix everything kicked off for him. Compare her own recording, {{ <track_link source="fernanda-porto-sambassim.md" /> }}.

The rhythm is a {{ <concept_link name="breakbeat" /> }}: kick and snare placed in a skipping, syncopated pattern rather than on every beat, the engine of jungle and drum and bass. Samba is also built from layered, syncopated percussion, which is why the two fit so well. Listen to how the drums rattle and skip around the beat rather than marching on it.

The secret of the track's calm is its {{ <concept_link name="bassline" /> }}. While the drums run at full speed, the bass rolls in long, round notes, so the body feels a slow sway under the fast surface. That split between frantic drums and relaxed bass is the core idea of liquid, melodic drum and bass, and it gives the vocal room to float.

## Artistic Context

DJ Patife, born Wagner Ribeiro de Souza in São Paulo in 1976, started as a resident at the city's Arena Music Hall and hosted the radio show Bassline on Energia 97 from 1999. With contemporaries such as DJ Marky and the Drumagick duo he put Brazilian drum and bass on the international map: his first album, Sounds of Drum'n'bass, was praised by Muzik and Mixmag in 2000, and three of his tracks appeared on V Recordings' Brazil EP. His Sambassim remix was released by the São Paulo label Trama and collected on his 2001 mixed compilation Cool Steps – Drum'n'bass Grooves; DJ Marky made his own remix of the song as well. Fernanda Porto, born in Serra Negra in 1965, went on to release her debut album in 2002 on Trama; it sold more than 100,000 copies in Brazil and earned her a Latin Grammy nomination for Best New Artist.

## Impact and Reception

Wikipedia credits the Patife version with making Sambassim popular in Brazil and many European countries, and Deezer's biography of Patife says it was hugely popular on London dance floors. Treibhaus, presenting Porto's 2003 European tour, called it one of the highest-selling drum and bass singles of all time; no independent sales figures confirm that, but the remix remains a staple of liquid and Brazilian-flavoured sets decades later. It belongs on this playground because it shows how naturally samba and breakbeat fit together when someone gets the balance right. Put it on when the sun is out and let it carry you.
