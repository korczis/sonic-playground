## Genre and Mood

**Gin Wigmore** ends Gravel & Wine with **Singin' My Soul**, an acoustic ballad from a singer-songwriter better known for stomping. After an album of blues-rock swagger and shout-along hooks, it is melancholic, intimate and soulful: the same gravel in the voice, with the band's noise taken away.

## Song Summary

Wikipedia's track listing credits the song to Wigmore alone, unlike several co-writes on the album, and places it eleventh and last. Kelly Theobald of Beat Magazine called it beautifully mellow and sorrowful, a song that perfectly showcases her unique, smoky voice, and joked that this is the track where the wine of the title is needed most, to drown her sorrows along with your own. For the blogger Paul Cole, the stripped-back closer revealed a surprising vulnerability in a singer who otherwise plays it tough.

## Musical Breakdown

No published analysis maps the song part by part, so this follows what the reviews describe.

### Intro

Luisa Mercier of Femme Metal Webzine heard it as another acoustic ballad, very, very melancholic, and it arrives straight after the up-tempo country of Sweet Hell, so the change in volume is the first thing to register.

### Main Section

The arrangement stays bare. With little around it, the voice carries the song: smoky, in Theobald's word, mellow rather than shouted, close enough to hear the grain.

### Outro

As the last track, its ending is the album's ending. The record closes on its quietest song rather than on another anthem.

## Concepts in Focus

A {{ <concept_link name="ballad" /> }} is a slow song built to carry feeling, and Singin' My Soul shows how little one needs. Compare it with {{ <track_link source="gin-wigmore-if-only.md" /> }} from the same album, where strings and broken-chord piano dress the slow tempo; here the reviewers heard only an acoustic setting.

That bareness is a choice of {{ <concept_link name="arrangement" /> }}. Gravel & Wine was made with a full band, and taking it away does not make the song smaller; it moves the voice to the front, where every rasp and held breath is audible. Cole's phrase, turning the volume dial down, describes an arrangement decision as much as a mood.

The song was never a single, which makes it a {{ <concept_link name="deep cut" /> }}: the album released Black Sheep and Man Like That as singles, yet the critics who wrote about the record still stopped to single this one out.

## Artistic Context

Wigmore, from New Zealand, recorded Gravel & Wine in Santa Monica in the second quarter of 2011 with the producer Butch Walker and his band, The Black Widows, after two months travelling through Mississippi and Alabama in search of the American South. The album came out in New Zealand on 7 November 2011 on Universal, debuted at number one there and went double platinum; it reached the United States in April 2013.

## Impact and Reception

The album divided critics: the New Zealand Herald's Lydia Jenkins gave it four and a half stars, while Sputnikmusic called it a kitchen-sink piece of pastiche. On its closer the reviews quoted here agree: mellow, melancholic, vulnerable. Play the album's opener first, then this, and hear how far one voice travels in eleven songs.
