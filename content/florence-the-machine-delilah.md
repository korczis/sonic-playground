## Genre and Mood

**Florence + The Machine**'s **Delilah** is pop with soul and gospel in its blood. Wikipedia files it under pop, gospel and Northern soul, and the song moves through all three: nervous at first, then defiant, and finally exuberant, a big voice deciding to dance instead of wait.

## Song Summary

Florence Welch wrote "Delilah" with her bandmate and songwriting partner Isabella Summers at Geejam studios in Jamaica, one of the first songs the pair wrote for the album. Welch has said it draws on a party they had just been to in Miami, the biblical tale of Samson and Delilah, and what she called the agony of the mobile phone in modern relationships. Songfacts traces the lyric to an anxious wait for a call from a boyfriend that never came. The arrangement, produced by Markus Dravs, starts small and keeps growing until the waiting turns into movement.

## Musical Breakdown

### Intro

The song begins sparsely: Welch's voice, multi-tracked so that she calls and answers herself, over a simple piano and what Rolling Stone's Jon Blistein called simmering synths.

### First Crescendo

The first build does something unexpected. Instead of exploding, it tapers back, and Welch slips into her falsetto, holding the tension a little longer.

### Chorus

Then the song reaches what Blistein called its exuberant, gospel-tinged core. Welch belts the line about never knowing she was a dancer until Delilah showed her how, and the full band, whose credits include organ, electric guitar, bass and drums, carries the groove.

### Outro

No source walks through the ending in detail. The album version runs just under five minutes, and the chorus, which Blistein heard Welch belting defiantly throughout, carries it home.

## Concepts in Focus

{{ <concept_link name="call and response" /> }} usually needs a leader and a group. Here Welch plays both parts: overdubbed lines answer her lead, so the opening sounds like a conversation inside one anxious head.

Her {{ <concept_link name="falsetto" /> }} matters at the turn of the first crescendo. By going lighter just when the song could get louder, she saves the full force of her voice for the gospel release that follows.

The {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }}, directed by Vincent Haycock, closed The Odyssey, a series of linked films. Welch roams a maze of motel rooms, cuts a man's hair in a nod to the biblical Delilah, and finally rides through the streets of Los Angeles. Haycock said the ending was about her returning to her true self.

## Artistic Context

"Delilah" comes from How Big, How Blue, How Beautiful, the band's third album, released by Island in May 2015 and their third consecutive UK number one. Will Hermes of Rolling Stone heard Welch leaning into classic rock and soul on it, and pictured her on "Delilah" time-travelling to party with the Old Testament femme fatale over a Northern soul groove. Compare the quieter {{ <track_link source="florence-the-machine-caught.md" /> }} from the same record.

## Impact and Reception

Released as the album's fourth and final single on 27 November 2015, it reached number 15 on Billboard's Hot Rock Songs and has been certified silver in the UK and gold in Brazil. Put it on and listen for the moment the crescendo pulls back.
