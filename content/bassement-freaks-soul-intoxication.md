## Genre and Mood

**Soul Intoxication (Lonely Boy's Detox Dub)** is a house dub of a track by **Basement Freaks**, the alias of the Thessaloniki producer George Fotiadis. The original comes from his 2013 album Funk From The Trunk on Jalapeno Records, a label known for funk, breaks and ghetto funk; the remix by the Los Angeles disco and house producer Lonely Boy takes it somewhere slower and deeper. The mood is introspective and hypnotic, and the "Detox" in the title promises what a dub mix does anyway: it takes things away.

## Song Structure and Sound Design

### Introduction

A dub mix starts lean. Instead of announcing the song, it lets the rhythm section establish itself and saves the recognisable material from the original for later.

### Build-up

Elements arrive one at a time over the beat, so the track gains depth by layering rather than by getting louder or faster.

### Main Section

The core is a steady house groove under fragments of the original. This is the "dub" in the title: parts of the record are muted, repeated or pushed into echo, and the space between them becomes part of the music.

### Breakdown

The beat pulls back for a stretch and the remaining sounds hang in the air on their own, a classic dub move of dropping instruments out of the mix.

### Climax

When the groove returns in full, the effect comes from the contrast with the breakdown rather than from any new material.

### Outro

The elements are withdrawn again until little more than the rhythm is left, the kind of ending DJs use to mix into the next record.

## Concepts in Focus

This is a {{ <concept_link name="remix" /> }} in the most practical sense. When Jalapeno Records lifted Soul Intoxication from the album as a single in August 2013, it commissioned three reworkings for three audiences: one by the Los Angeles act Skaggs, a "supremely summery house mix" by the Australian breaks producer Tom Drummond, and this one by Lonely Boy. The same song is pointed at different dance floors by different producers.

The title calls it a dub, which in house music means a version built on the drums and bass with most of the song stripped out. The term comes straight from Jamaican {{ <concept_link name="dub" /> }}, where engineers such as King Tubby and Lee "Scratch" Perry remixed reggae records live at the mixing desk, muting parts, adding echo and letting instruments drop in and out. A house dub keeps that habit of subtraction: listen for what is missing as much as for what is there.

Underneath it sits a {{ <concept_link name="house groove" /> }}, steady and unhurried, which moves the track away from the funk and breakbeat that Basement Freaks are known for. Compare {{ <track_link source="groove-armada-superstylin.md" /> }}, another record where dub's sense of space meets a house beat.

## Production Techniques

Dub production rests on the mixing desk as an instrument: echo and reverb sends opened and closed by hand, filters swept across a part, whole instruments muted for a few bars and brought back. Those are the tools the "Detox Dub" name points to. The original album version is credited to George Fotiadis as composer; the remix is credited to Lonely Boy.

## Thematic Interpretation

The title sets intoxication against detox, and the remix acts that contrast out. The album version is a funk-rooted track; the dub empties it out until what is left is pulse and space. Whether you hear that as sobering up or as going deeper into the trance is up to the listener, and the repetition supports either.

## Cultural Context

The track shows how a record can travel between scenes. Basement Freaks have released on breaks and funk labels such as Jalapeno, Bombastic Jam and Ghetto Funk; Lonely Boy works in disco and house; and in 2015 the French label Wagram Music placed this dub on two of its lounge compilations, Saint-Germain-des-Prés Café 17 and Deep Lounge 02. A single written for funk dance floors ended up as the soundtrack of Paris café playlists.

## Potential Impact and Reception

Its afterlife on lounge compilations says what the remix is good at: it works on a club system and as late-evening background alike. It is here as a lesson in how much a remixer can change by taking things away. Put it on with the original album version next to it and listen for what Lonely Boy left out.
