## Genre and Mood

**Depeche Mode** were a synthesizer band when they released **Personal Jesus** in August 1989, and it sounded like something else: a guitar riff, a stamping beat and a lyric that mixes the sacred with the sexual. Wikipedia files it under synth-pop, alternative rock and blues rock at once. The mood is dark and provocative.

## Song Summary

Martin Gore wrote it after reading Priscilla Presley's memoir Elvis and Me, and described it as a song about being a Jesus for somebody else, about how people become gods to each other in love. Gore's demo was simple: a melody on acoustic guitar while he tapped the beat with his foot. In the studio Alan Wilder and the producer Flood sent the rest of the band away and built it into the band's first guitar-forward single. This is the single version, under four minutes, the one on The Best of Depeche Mode, Volume 1; the album version on Violator runs almost five.

## Musical Breakdown

No source times the track, so this follows its documented layers.

### The Stomp

The pulse comes from a sample: the band recorded people jumping on road cases. On Violator most percussion was sampled and looped rather than programmed on a drum machine, to keep a more human feel.

### The Riff

Over it sits the guitar riff grown from Gore's demo; Flood heard the track as a way of saying, here is Depeche Mode, but not as you know them.

### The Vocals

Dave Gahan sings the lead. In Music Week, David Giles praised its vocal harmonies, sung an octave apart, and called the song stark and foreboding.

## Concepts in Focus

A {{ <concept_link name="riff" /> }} is a short repeated figure that a song is built on. Here it was a statement: guitars had appeared on earlier Depeche Mode records, but never at the front of a single. Compare {{ <track_link source="the-white-stripes-seven-nation-army.md" /> }}, another song that lives on one figure.

{{ <concept_link name="sampling" /> }} usually means borrowing from records; here the band sampled their own world, the thud of bodies on flight cases, and looped it into a beat.

The {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }} by Anton Corbijn was shot in the Tabernas Desert in Almería, with the band as cowboys and shots of women suggesting a brothel; MTV censored it.

The song invited a {{ <concept_link name="cover version" /> }}. Johnny Cash recorded it as stripped-down acoustic blues for the album that also holds {{ <track_link source="johnny-cash-hurt.md" /> }}; Marilyn Manson's 2004 version reached number 13 in the UK.

## Artistic Context

Personal Jesus came out on Mute more than six months before Violator, which was recorded in Milan and Denmark and released in March 1990. Newspaper advertisements offered readers their own personal Jesus and a phone number that played the song; some British papers refused to print them.

## Impact and Reception

It reached number 13 in the UK and number 28 in the US, the band's first American top 40 hit since People Are People and their first gold single there. By 2006 it had sold a million copies, and Rolling Stone placed it among the 500 greatest songs of all time. Turn it up and feel the road cases.
