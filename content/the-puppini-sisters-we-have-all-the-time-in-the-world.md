## Genre and Mood

**The Puppini Sisters** end their second album with **We Have All The Time In The World**, and they end it slowly. It is a jazz ballad and a cover, sung by a close-harmony trio in the manner of the 1940s: romantic, sultry and nostalgic twice over, for an old film song and an older way of singing.

## Song Summary

John Barry wrote the music and Hal David the words for On Her Majesty's Secret Service in 1969, and Louis Armstrong sang it. The title is James Bond's last line in the film, spoken after the death of his wife Tracy, and Barry chose Armstrong because he felt he could deliver it with irony. The Puppini Sisters keep the melody and the words but change the weight. George Graham heard them take it as a slow ballad backed by a string section; Anna Peska heard a creamy, smooth, sultry, loved-up close, with Marcella Puppini on accordion.

### The Voices

Marcella Puppini, Kate Mullins and Stephanie O'Brien sing in close harmony, the sound the trio is built on.

### The Strings

A string section carries the accompaniment, in an arrangement credited to David Davidson.

### The Accordion

One review singles out Marcella Puppini's accordion here; listen for it among the strings.

## Concepts in Focus

A {{ <concept_link name="cover version" /> }} can be faithful or transform a song, and this one does a little of both. The trio often reimagine contemporary songs in the style of the Andrews Sisters, whom their name honours; this song is already old, so the change is in the voices: three instead of one.

That change is {{ <concept_link name="vocal harmony" /> }} at its closest. Graham described the album's style as that of the 1940s female groups with their vocal harmonies, and a slow ballad leaves that blend nowhere to hide. Compare {{ <track_link source="l-e-j-hanging-tree.md" /> }}, where another trio sings a film song over strings.

The song also comes from a {{ <concept_link name="soundtrack" /> }}, and it keeps something of the film. Its title is a promise of time that the story immediately takes away, which is why Barry wanted irony in the voice.

## Artistic Context

Marcella Puppini, who was born in Italy and studied music at Trinity College of Music in London, founded the trio in London in 2004, inspired by the film The Triplets of Belleville. The Rise and Fall of Ruby Woo, named after the group's favourite lipstick, came out on 1 October 2007 on Universal Classics and Jazz, produced by Martin Terefe, Jon Hall and the group. Its sequence moves from upbeat songs to bittersweet ones and closes with this track.

## Impact and Reception

In The Guardian, Liz Hoggard wrote that if you really want to wallow, you should go straight to this song, adding that you would not hear a more exquisite track all year. The album reached number 73 in the UK, number 9 on Billboard's Jazz Albums chart and number 43 in France. Listen to Armstrong's original first, then to this one.
