## Genre and Mood

**Mount Sims** spent the early 2000s as one of electroclash's sleekest provocateurs, but **New Authority Volunteers** belongs to his darker, guitar-driven turn. It sits in post-punk and darkwave territory, alternative in spirit, and the mood is dark, rebellious and gritty, a short burst of unease rather than a dancefloor tease.

## Song Summary

The title alone reads like a poster for some sinister militia, and the song leans into that atmosphere of control and resistance. Instead of the glossy synths of his earlier records, the sound is built from tense guitars, insistent percussion and a vocal that sits somewhere between croon and command. It is compact, just over three minutes, and wastes none of them. The memorable quality is its tension: it never fully releases.

## Musical Breakdown

The track is direct and economical, in the tradition of the post-punk singles it draws from.

### Intro

Guitars and drums start up with a cold, driving momentum, setting a mood that is closer to a rehearsal room than a club.

### Verse 1

The vocal enters with a detached, slightly menacing calm, delivering lines over a beat that never loosens its grip.

### Chorus

The hook tightens the screws rather than releasing them, the title chanted like a slogan.

### Outro

The song cuts off with the same abruptness it began with, leaving the unease hanging in the air.

## Artistic Context

Mount Sims is the project of American musician Matthew Sims, who rose to attention through DJ Hell's International DeeJay Gigolo label with the electroclash album UltraSex in 2002. His later records moved steadily away from dance music: reviews of his 2008 album Happily Ever After described a stark, guitar-and-percussion post-punk record with guests from Swans and the Berlin underground, and cited darkwave and goth influences such as DAF and The Chameleons. "New Authority Volunteers" comes from Happily Ever After... Again, a release in that same orbit.

## Impact and Reception

Critics were divided on that darker phase, some praising its singing and atmosphere, others missing his electro wit. "New Authority Volunteers" is on this playground because it shows the other side of a familiar name: the same cool detachment, now aimed at something more ominous. It also makes a fascinating companion to his earlier hits: put it next to the electroclash of UltraSex and you can hear an artist deliberately trading polish for grit. Play it loud on a grey afternoon and let it rattle you.
