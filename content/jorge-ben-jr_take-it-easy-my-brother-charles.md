## Genre and Mood

From the very first notes of **Take It Easy My Brother Charles**, Jorge Ben sets an effortlessly cool groove. A swinging samba guitar, crisp percussion and his rich, relaxed voice make a song that feels like a sunny afternoon, even though its message is more serious than it sounds.

## Song Summary

There is a lightness to the song, but it carries a message. Ben addresses Charles, a "brother of color", with words of caution, partly in English; the scholar Christopher Dunn has called it a prime example of soul music's ethos of racial pride, self-determination and collective struggle. The music, samba with the funk and soul that were reaching Brazil at the end of the 1960s, keeps the tone warm rather than angry. It is over in about two and a half minutes.

## Lyrics Breakdown

### Intro: Setting the Scene

The track opens with Ben's swinging acoustic guitar and percussion, establishing the samba groove at once.

### Verse 1: Embracing the Groove

In the first verse Ben speaks to Charles in a relaxed, conversational way, mostly in Portuguese, his voice riding the rhythm rather than pushing against it.

### Chorus: A Call to Relaxation

The chorus brings the English title line, "take it easy", a brotherly word of caution rather than an invitation to laziness.

### Verse 2: The Essence of Leisure

The second verse continues the address to Charles over the same easy, syncopated groove.

### Bridge: The Heartbeat of Samba

The percussion and José Briamonte's arrangement fill out the sound, showing the samba rhythm that underpins the whole track.

### Final Chorus: Celebration of Life

The final chorus returns to the title line, and the song ends while the groove is still rolling.

## Concepts in Focus

The {{ <concept_link name="groove" /> }} is the song. Jorge Ben's guitar playing, rhythmic and percussive rather than chordal decoration, locks with the percussion of Trio Mocotó, who accompanied him on the album, into a pulse that sways and pushes at once. Rodney Taylor heard funk in it; you can hear soul too, but the engine is samba.

That swing comes from {{ <concept_link name="syncopation" /> }}: accents that land just ahead of or between the main beats. Listen to the guitar's strokes and the way the voice places its words slightly off the pulse; take the accents away and the song would sound square. The same samba rhythm, transformed by machines, drives {{ <track_link source="fernanda-porto-sambassim.md" /> }}.

## Artistic Context

Jorge Ben (who has recorded as Jorge Ben Jor since 1989) released *Jorge Ben*, his sixth studio album, in November 1969 on Philips Records. It was recorded at C.B.D. in Rio de Janeiro and Scatena in São Paulo, produced by Manoel Barenbein, with Trio Mocotó and orchestral arrangements by José Briamonte and Rogério Duprat; Briamonte arranged this track. Ben wrote every song on the album, which also includes "País Tropical" and "Charles, Anjo 45", another song about a man named Charles.
