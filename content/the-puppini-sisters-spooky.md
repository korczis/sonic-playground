## Genre and Mood

**The Puppini Sisters** make **Spooky** sound older than it is. The song is a sixties pop hit; their version files it under jazz and vocal pop, swinging like a forties dance band. The mood is playful and sultry at once, nostalgic for an era the song never belonged to.

## Song Summary

Spooky began in 1967 as a saxophone instrumental by Mike Sharpe, written by Sharpe (Mike Shapiro) and Harry Middlebrooks Jr. A year later James Cobb and Buddy Buie gave it words about a spooky little girl for Classics IV, whose version reached number 3 in the US; Dusty Springfield recorded a famous version of her own. The Puppini Sisters chose it to open their second album and released it as its first single in 2007. The melody that Dennis Yost once sang alone for Classics IV now belongs to three voices.

## Musical Breakdown

No source walks through this recording section by section; what the sources describe is the trio's voices and the feel.

### Main Section

A melody written for a single lead singer meets the Puppinis' signature, close harmony, which amNY described as three voices singing just a couple of notes apart. That blend, not any one voice, is what carries their records.

### Groove

Where the Classics IV recording sounded to one listener like California in the sixties, here the same writer heard no California at all, only a definite big-band swing feel.

## Concepts in Focus

As a {{ <concept_link name="cover version" /> }}, Spooky shows how far a song can travel. Its melody has survived a saxophone, a male pop singer, Dusty Springfield and now a trio; each version keeps the tune and changes the era it seems to come from.

The tool here is close {{ <concept_link name="vocal harmony" /> }} in the tradition of the Andrews Sisters, the group the Puppinis' name salutes, and amNY called it the backbone of their musical identity.

The rhythmic change is {{ <concept_link name="swing" /> }}: notes played long-short rather than evenly, the lilt of forties big bands. Applied to a sixties song, it pulls the whole record decades back.

## Artistic Context

Marcella Puppini founded the trio in London in 2004 after seeing the animated film The Triplets of Belleville, and Universal Classics and Jazz signed them in 2005. The Rise and Fall of Ruby Woo, released on 1 October 2007 and produced by Martin Terefe, Jon Hall and the group, mixes old standards, contemporary hits reworked in the Andrews Sisters' style, such as Walk Like an Egyptian and Crazy in Love, and originals. The trio then was Puppini, Kate Mullins and Stephanie O'Brien.

## Impact and Reception

The album reached the top ten of the US jazz albums chart and number 43 in France. BBC Music still found it gimmicky but said the songs sounded silky and sexy; amNY heard the group moving beyond reproduction to something quirkier. In 2009 they sang Spooky in the Jonathan Creek special The Grinning Man. Put it next to the Classics IV original and listen to how the swing changes everything.
