## Genre and Mood

**Dave Bixby**'s **Drug Song** is folk at its barest: one young man, an acoustic guitar and a tape machine's echo. The echo pushes it towards psychedelic music, and the subject keeps it melancholic and introspective, and the voice at its centre is one a critic called haunting.

## Song Summary

Drug Song opens Ode to Quetzalcoatl, the album Bixby recorded in 1969 in a living room in Grand Rapids, Michigan, with voice, acoustic guitar and reel-to-reel echo. Spectrum Culture's Pat Padua called it a cautionary song in which Bixby tells the true story of his own struggle with addiction. Bixby, asked decades later whether it was a confession, a warning or an honest account, answered that it was all of those but mostly regret: he had thought he was alone in the fight for his soul.

### Main Section

Bixby sings in what Padua heard as a haunting tenor. Will Pinfold, reviewing the album for the same site in 2025, heard echo-laden acoustic guitar and yearning, droning vocals throughout, recorded intimately on a four-track machine. No source describes how the song is divided into verses.

## Concepts in Focus

Ode to Quetzalcoatl is {{ <concept_link name="lo-fi" /> }} by circumstance, not by fashion: a 1969 private recording made at home on a limited budget, with no commercial aim. Bixby put it simply: he was not performing, he was expressing.

Its signature is {{ <concept_link name="delay" /> }}, here the echo of a reel-to-reel tape machine. Pinfold argued that without that ubiquitous echo the album would sound flat and harsh; with it, the album sounds trippy.

The song also travelled through {{ <concept_link name="sampling" /> }}: when Travis Scott sampled it on Parasail, Bixby received a writing credit.

## Artistic Context

Bixby, born in Grand Rapids in 1950, played in the garage band Peter and the Prophets before going solo in local coffeehouses. In 1968 he embraced the counterculture; near-daily LSD use and a deep depression he called the void followed, and he turned to Christianity. The album documents that passage. He later joined a religious group led by Don DeGraaf, recorded as Harbinger, and left in 1974.

## Impact and Reception

Pressed in limited quantities, the album gained a cult following among collectors; Guerssen issued its first authorised reissue in 2009. In 2011 MGMT put Drug Song on their Late Night Tales compilation. Pinfold, less convinced, gave the album 66 per cent. Listen late at night, alone, as it was made.
