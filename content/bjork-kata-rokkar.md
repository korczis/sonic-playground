## Genre and Mood

**Kata rokkar** ("Kata rocks") is not the electronic, experimental **Björk** most listeners know. It comes from Gling-Gló, the 1990 album she recorded with the Guðmundur Ingólfsson Trio: vocal jazz and Icelandic popular song, played by an acoustic piano trio with her voice up front. The mood is playful and warm, with a nostalgic glow: the inlay text by the Icelandic songwriter Megas describes the album as fifties jazz-pop meeting the present.

## Song Summary

The song was written by the Icelandic songwriter Theodór Einarsson, and like most of Gling-Gló it is sung in Icelandic. It is short, a little under three minutes, and built for fun rather than depth, and the title alone, "Kata rocks", tells you it is meant to be delivered with a grin. What makes it worth hearing is the contrast with everything that came after. Here Björk's voice is barely processed, sitting in a small room with three musicians, and its character, the quick shifts from childlike lightness to a rougher edge, is already unmistakable.

## Musical Breakdown

### Intro

The trio opens on its own: piano, bass and drums, with no synthesizers or studio effects. Guðmundur Ingólfsson's piano leads.

### Vocal Entry

Björk enters close to the microphone, phrasing the Icelandic lyric lightly and rhythmically, with the bounce the title promises.

### Main Section

Voice and piano trade lines while bass and drums keep the time steady underneath, the traditional division of labour in a jazz trio.

### Climax

The energy peaks through the performance rather than the arrangement: Björk pushes her voice harder and the trio plays with her, without any added layers.

### Outro

The song wraps up quickly and cleanly, more like a number in a live set than a produced single.

## Concepts in Focus

Gling-Gló is an album of {{ <concept_link name="cover version" /> }}s and interpretations. Björk did not write Kata rokkar; Theodór Einarsson did, and the record sets it next to jazz standards translated into Icelandic and older popular songs reworked for the trio. A cover can be faithful or transforming, and either way it tells you about the interpreter: here, a singer from the Reykjavík punk and Sugarcubes scene showing that she knew and loved her country's songbook.

The trio plays in the jazz tradition, and its rhythm leans on {{ <concept_link name="swing" /> }}, the uneven, lilting division of the beat that makes jazz, boogie-woogie and early rock 'n' roll feel loose rather than mechanical. All About Jazz singled out the boogie-woogie and bop-style exchanges between Ingólfsson's piano and the vocals as the album's liveliest moments. Compare the larger, more polished vocal-jazz band behind {{ <track_link source="gregory-porter-do-not-lose-your-steam.md" /> }}.

## Artistic Context

Gling-Gló was released in 1990 on Smekkleysa, the Reykjavík label of the Sugarcubes circle, credited to Björk Guðmundsdóttir & tríó Guðmundar Ingólfssonar. The trio was pianist Guðmundur Ingólfsson, bassist Þórður Högnason and drummer Guðmundur Steingrímsson, and the album was produced and mixed by Tómas Magnús Tómasson, the bassist of Stuðmenn. Most of it was recorded in two days, on 1 and 3 September 1990 at Stúdio Sýrland; two songs come from a radio session for the national broadcaster RÚV. Its title is the Icelandic equivalent of "ding dong", the sound of a bell. Björk was still a member of the Sugarcubes; her solo album Debut followed in 1993.

## Impact and Reception

Gling-Gló received mixed reviews and remains a side road in Björk's catalogue, though it has been reissued outside Iceland several times since 1990. All About Jazz called it a pretty good, playful record whose minimally processed vocals and simple trio would surprise fans of her later work, and praised Ingólfsson's piano most. Kata rokkar is one of its brightest moments, and a useful starting point for anyone curious about where that voice came from.
