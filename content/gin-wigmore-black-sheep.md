## Genre and Mood

**Gin Wigmore** sings **Black Sheep** with her chin up and her boots on. It is rock with blues grit and a soul singer's swagger, a stomping, handclapping anthem that is defiant, rebellious and bold from its first beat to its last shout.

## Song Summary

The song is a proud declaration of not fitting in, a celebration of the outsider who stopped apologising for it. Wigmore's voice, raspy and powerful with a rough, smoky edge, sits on top of a stomping rhythm, twanging guitar and gang vocals that make the whole thing feel like a crowd marching behind her. The production borrows from vintage rock and roll and blues stompers, but the attitude is entirely her own.

## Musical Breakdown

The track is short, direct and built for impact, with each section raising the stakes.

### Intro

A heavy stomp-and-clap beat kicks things off, a rhythm you can feel in your chest before the guitars arrive.

### Verse 1

Wigmore enters with a low, gravelly growl, laying out her case as the family outcast with a wink rather than a complaint.

### Chorus

The chorus explodes into a shout-along hook, the title worn like a badge, with backing voices answering her as if the whole flock of misfits has joined in.

### Bridge

The arrangement pulls back to the beat and the voice, a moment of swagger before the final charge.

### Climax

The last chorus hits hardest, her voice at full rasp and the band stomping behind her.

### Outro

The song ends on a punchy final hit rather than a fade, as blunt as its message.

## Artistic Context

Gin Wigmore is a singer-songwriter from Auckland, New Zealand. "Black Sheep" was the lead single from her second album, Gravel & Wine, released in 2011 through Universal and produced with Butch Walker, who is also credited as a co-writer. The album leaned into vintage soul, blues and rock and roll.

## Impact and Reception

Released in September 2011, "Black Sheep" debuted and peaked at number 13 on the New Zealand chart and also charted in Switzerland. Its afterlife on screen has been bigger still: it was used in a trailer for Orange Is the New Black, appeared in several TV series and soundtracked a car commercial in 2017.
