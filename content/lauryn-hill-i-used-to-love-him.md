## Genre and Mood

**Lauryn Hill** and Mary J. Blige share **I Used to Love Him**, and the title misleads. It sounds like a lament, yet Jennifer Adetoro of Dazed called it one of the greatest break-up songs ever written and super uplifting: a song about a love that dulled the senses and kept the singer from herself. R&B, soul and hip-hop at once, it is soulful, reflective and finally empowering.

## Song Summary

The song is track nine of sixteen on The Miseducation of Lauryn Hill and runs 5:39. Wikipedia, drawing on the biographer Chris Nickson, reads it as a narrator looking back on a finished relationship with detachment and self-criticism. Producer Vada Nobles told Rolling Stone, as MadameNoire reports, that it came out of a conversation about Hill's own relationship in the small studio in her attic in South Orange, and that it was about Wyclef Jean.

## Musical Breakdown

No timed analysis maps its sections; this follows its makers and its credits.

### The Groove

Rasheem "Kilo" Pugh of the New Ark collective told XXL the beat was made in that attic, with Nobles pacing and humming while Hill wrote. He called it one of the easiest records, with fewer instruments: a real jazzy groove, like that hip-hop jazz.

### The Voices

Hill sings the lead and Blige the featured vocal, with eight backing singers credited. Jo, one of New Ark's twins Johari and Tejumold Newton, put the invitation simply: Hill wanted Mary on the album because Mary was her homegirl.

### The Turn

The Dissect podcast hears Hill hand her life over to God here, leading straight into Forgive Them Father.

## Concepts in Focus

Blige is a {{ <concept_link name="featured artist" /> }} in the fullest sense: a star in her own right, credited on the track as Lauryn Hill feat. Mary J. Blige, singing beside the host rather than delivering a guest verse. The student critic Jasmine McIntosh singled out the sweet combination of their voices and wished they had made more songs together.

The song is also a {{ <concept_link name="deep cut" /> }}. MadameNoire calls it exactly that; the album's three singles were Doo Wop (That Thing), Ex-Factor and Everything Is Everything. Its meaning grows from its place in a {{ <concept_link name="concept album" /> }} about educating oneself on love: Nickson hears it as the logical follow-up to When It Hurts So Bad, and Forgive Them Father follows naturally from it.

## Artistic Context

Ruffhouse and Columbia released the album on 25 August 1998 in the United States. Hill is credited as its writer and producer, and the label's metadata also names D.J. Rogers as composer and lyricist of this song. The New Ark musicians later sued over their credits, and the case was settled out of court in 2001. Compare another late-1990s meeting of rap and soul, {{ <track_link source="the-roots-erykah-badu-you-got-me.md" /> }}.

## Impact and Reception

The album won Album of the Year and Best R&B Album at the 41st Grammy Awards, among Hill's five wins that night. Not everyone close to it loved this song: Tej, the other twin, told XXL it was the one he liked least. Hear it between its neighbours, and a break-up becomes a release.
