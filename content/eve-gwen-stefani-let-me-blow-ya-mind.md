## Genre and Mood

**Eve** and **Gwen Stefani** met halfway on **Let Me Blow Ya Mind**, a hip-hop track with an R&B hook and a pop star singing it. Eve wanted it to feel "fun and cocky and celebratory", and that is the mood: confident, playful, a victory lap taken at an easy walk.

## Song Summary

The lyric answers the people who doubted Eve could stay popular in the mainstream; she said it announced that she was in the music business to stay. It was the second single from her second album, Scorpion, released by Ruff Ryders and Interscope on 2 April 2001. Dr. Dre and Scott Storch produced it, and Eve called it one song that she wrote fully; Dre, she said, kept her in the studio until it was done. Wikipedia gives it a tempo of 90 BPM.

## Musical Breakdown

No source walks through the song part by part; these notes follow what reviewers heard in it.

### Intro

No source describes the opening bars; what reviewers heard is the beat beneath everything. One retrospective review hears the drums as a simple stomp back and forth between kick and snare, calculated and spacious, and the keys as having a world to fill.

### Verse 1

Eve raps over that open ground. The Cornell Daily Sun found the album's revelation in her singing as much as her rapping, and on this song it shows: she does both.

### Chorus

On the hook, Eve duets with Stefani, and the Sun wrote that she even holds her own beside her. The Indiana Daily Student was less kind, hearing a practically invisible Stefani and a syncopated hook that reminded it of OutKast's Ms. Jackson.

## Concepts in Focus

The {{ <concept_link name="featured artist" /> }} here was a gamble. Eve, a long-time No Doubt fan, had a gut feeling the two would sound great together; others told her it would never work. Interscope's Jimmy Iovine paired Stefani with Dre for the song.

A {{ <concept_link name="hook" /> }} that rides {{ <concept_link name="syncopation" /> }}, landing between the beats rather than on them, is what the Indiana reviewer heard, and it is why the line sticks even for someone who disliked it.

The {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }}, directed by Philip Atwell, sends Eve and Stefani crashing a formal party until both are arrested, with cameos from Udo Kier, Jadakiss and Styles P; Dr. Dre pays their bail at the end. It won the 2001 MTV Video Music Award for Best Female Video.

## Artistic Context

Eve had been signed to Dr. Dre's Aftermath and dropped after eight months; a second deal with Ruff Ryders brought her back to him. The retrospective review quoted above hears Dre, fresh from his own 2001 and {{ <track_link source="dr-dre-let-s-get-high.md" /> }}, perfecting his brand of minimalism with Storch here.

## Impact and Reception

The single reached number two on the Billboard Hot 100, Eve's highest placing there, number four in the UK and number one in Ireland, Norway, Switzerland and both halves of Belgium. In 2002 it won the first Grammy for Best Rap/Sung Collaboration, the first Grammy for both women. Listen for how little is in the beat, and how much the two voices do with it.
