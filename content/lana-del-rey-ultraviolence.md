## Genre and Mood

**Lana Del Rey**'s **Ultraviolence** is a rock ballad that moves slowly and speaks softly about something violent. The mood is dark, haunting and melancholic, and the song's unease comes from the gap between a lush, almost devotional sound and a story of a love that hurts.

## Song Summary

The narrator stays with a difficult man named Jim, and the chorus borrows from the Crystals' 1962 He Hit Me (And It Felt Like a Kiss), a song already controversial in its day. MTV News summed up the relationship as broken, failed and painful, typical of her songs. Dan Auerbach of the Black Keys produced and mixed it at his Easy Eye Sound studio in Nashville, with a band of guitarists, pedal steel, piano and mellotron, bass and drums.

## Musical Breakdown

### The Arrangement

Critics agreed on the slow pace and the strings. Slant Magazine's Sal Cinquemani called it a laconic, string-laden torch song; Radio.com heard Del Rey's lilting, low-tempo, strings-heavy form pushed further; the Music Times picked out violins, lightly thumping drums and her angelic singing, which gave it a church-like feel.

### The Verses

KEXP's Gerrit Feenstra heard the opening lines frame a teacher-and-student dynamic in harsh, physical language, the album's central theme introduced in the manner of Anthony Burgess's A Clockwork Orange.

### The Chorus

The chorus carries the reference to the Crystals' song. No source describes how the song opens, builds or ends.

## Concepts in Focus

The Crystals reference is an {{ <concept_link name="interpolation" /> }}: a line from an older song sung anew rather than sampled from its recording. Del Rey had also heard the song in a version by Hole, so the line reaches her through two generations of reinterpretation.

Ultraviolence is a rock {{ <concept_link name="ballad" /> }}, slow and, as critics heard it, wrapped in strings; that tenderness is what makes its lyric hard to hold at a distance.

The {{ <concept_link name="record producer" /> }} shaped the record. Auerbach was brought in to rework an album Del Rey had considered finished; three planned days became six weeks, and he said she sang every song live, with a handheld microphone and a seven-piece band. KEXP heard his arrangements lean towards guitar-driven blues and the space between notes; Stereogum later called the album much less maximalist than Born to Die.

## Artistic Context

Ultraviolence was released on 4 June 2014 as the third single from the album of the same name, issued by Polydor and Interscope on 13 June. The album debuted at number one on the Billboard 200, Del Rey's first US number-one album. Its {{ <concept_link name="music video" /> }}, directed by Francesco Carrozzini, was shot on an iPhone with an 8mm camera app in Portofino and shows her in a wedding dress, later entering a chapel.

## Impact and Reception

The song reached number 70 on the Billboard Hot 100 and number 38 in Canada and was later certified platinum in the US. Its subject divided critics: the Guardian's Harriet Gibson said it appeared to romanticise brutality, and Time's Nolan Feeney criticised it for glorifying domestic violence, while NME still named it the 32nd best song of 2014. Listen to it with that argument in mind, and notice how gently it is sung.
